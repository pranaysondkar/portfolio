The result: 93% reduction in manual effort. $48K annual savings. Completed ahead of schedule. Full documentation so it would not break after I left. The tool worked. But the trust we built together is what made it possible.

Lessons

What experience taught me.

These are not textbook principles. They are hard-won lessons from real projects, real teams, and real deadlines.

01
Earn the right to innovate
Nobody cares about your great idea if you have not proven you can handle the basics. My manager did not trust my automation pitch. She trusted that I would not let the daily work suffer. Credibility comes before creativity.
02
Choose sustainable over impressive
I had ideas for Python scripts, SQL databases, custom applications. I scrapped them all. Because if my workflow breaks after I leave and nobody can troubleshoot it, I did not automate anything. I just created a different kind of problem. Low-code won because it was the right tool, not the cool one.
03
Most vendor problems are communication problems
I took vendor on-time delivery from 65% to 92%. Not with threats or escalations. With clarity. Clear criteria before work starts, checkpoints throughout, and treating vendors as partners instead of executors.
04
Start with process, not tools
Jira did not fix our delivery problems. Process did. When I inherited a team tracking work across spreadsheets, emails, and outdated files, leadership wanted "better tools." The real fix was one source of truth everyone trusted, weekly alignment, and visible accountability without micromanagement.
05
Documentation is a deliverable
A solution that breaks when you leave is not a solution. I built comprehensive troubleshooting guides for every automation I created. The goal was never to make myself indispensable. It was to make the work sustainable.
06
Give more than you take
Every team I have worked with invested in teaching me. The automation at State Street, the process improvements at Researchwire, the Scrum coaching at TCS. None of those were resume lines. They were ways of saying thank you.

AI should augment judgment, not replace it.

I had ideas for Python scripts, SQL databases, and custom applications at State Street. I scrapped them all. Because if my workflow breaks after I leave and nobody can troubleshoot it, I did not automate anything. I just created a different kind of problem. The same principle applies to AI. The question is never "can AI do this?" The question is "should AI do this, and will it still work when I am not in the room?"

I do not just use AI. I evaluate, implement, and govern it.

At State Street I built automation that saved $48K annually. Now I am extending that same mindset into AI: specifically how to evaluate which tools to adopt, how to implement them within existing project workflows, and how to govern their use so the organization does not end up with another unsustainable dependency. The engineer in me asks "how does this work?" The project manager asks "how does this scale?"
