Lessons

What experience taught me.

These are not textbook principles. They are lessons I learned by getting things wrong first, then figuring out why.

01
Front-load the hard work
Spend extra time at the start to build a real system. A process that everyone understands, a single source of truth that nobody questions. It feels slow in week one. By month three, you are the only team that is not firefighting. Your future self will thank you. Your current self will complain. Do it anyway.
02
Creating beats memorizing
I was an average student for most of my life. The kind of kid whose report card said "has potential" every single year. Then I started building things with my hands: robots, circuits, automation. The moment I stopped trying to remember answers and started solving problems, everything clicked. I bring that same instinct to project management. I do not look for templates. I look for what this specific team, this specific problem, actually needs.
03
Build a room where nobody is afraid
The best work happens when people feel safe enough to ask the obvious question. I want to build teams where no one performs confidence they do not have, where asking "I do not understand this" is respected, not punished. When your team looks at you for inspiration instead of approval, you are doing it right.
04
The connection you do not make is the opportunity you never see
This job search taught me something I should have learned earlier: relationships are not a networking strategy. They are how the world actually works. The referral that opens a door, the conversation that reframes your thinking, the person who remembers your name six months later. LinkedIn connections are not relationships. Coffee conversations are.
05
Stay until the lights go off
A project is not done when the deliverable ships. It is done when the team can run it without you. Handoffs, documentation, knowledge transfer: these are not administrative tasks at the end of a project. They are the actual finish line. If your solution dies when you leave the room, you did not finish.
06
Solve for the person, not the task
When a team member is stuck, the easy move is to assign the fix and move on. The better move is to understand why they are stuck. Is it a skill gap? A confidence gap? A communication gap? Nine times out of ten, the blocker is not the task itself. It is something around the task that nobody thought to ask about.

Trust, but verify. Every single time.

What worries me about AI in project management is not that it will replace PMs. It is that PMs will stop checking the output. A status report generated by AI that nobody reads before sending is not efficiency. It is negligence with extra steps. AI is a power tool. Power tools are dangerous when you stop paying attention.

Same instinct, new layer.

I have been automating things since I wired my first Arduino circuit. The tools changed: hardware to code, code to low-code, low-code to AI. But the instinct never did. Find the repetitive work. Understand it deeply enough to know what can be automated and what needs a human. Build the solution. Make sure it survives without you. AI is not a revolution in how I work. It is the next iteration.
